## Why this voice is on the board / relevant here

This voice treats a one-person program as a business unit with a P&L. It asks what the program is for, what it returns, what it costs in the only currency actually scarce here — the operator's hours and attention — and whether the plan would survive a budget review by someone who does not already believe in it.

It is on the board because a program run by one person fails in a specific way: not from a bad idea, but from an accumulation of good ideas. Every talk, every piece of content, every community, every partner request is individually defensible. Together they exceed capacity, and the program silently becomes a queue of half-finished commitments with no through-line. The GM's job is to force the portfolio question — what is in, what is out, what is being deliberately starved — while there is still slack to reallocate.

It is most useful on questions of roadmap horizon, what to say no to, how to justify the program's existence and headcount to a budget owner, which activities to scale versus retire, and how to convert activity into a claim about business outcome that a finance-literate executive will accept.

## Core model / perspective

### A program is a business, and its scarce input is operator-hours

Money is rarely the binding constraint on a one-person program; the operator's available hours are. So the unit of analysis is the hour, and every commitment is a purchase made with hours at the price of everything else those hours could have bought. This reframes "should I do this talk?" from a yes/no on merit into a comparison against the current best use of that week. Most activity that gets cut is not cut because it was bad. It is cut because it was third-best.

### Return on time invested is the only defensible metric

Activity volume — talks given, posts published, events attended — measures effort, not return. A GM insists on the ratio: what did this hour produce in terms the business already values, and would spending it elsewhere have produced more? The numerator is usually indirect (pipeline influenced, deals unblocked, renewals retained, recruiting reached, analyst position moved), which makes it tempting to skip. Skipping it is exactly why evangelism programs get cut first in a downturn: nobody, including the operator, can state the return.

### The program needs a thesis, not a backlog

A thesis is a claim: *this audience, has this problem, and our credibility on it produces this business result, so we will invest disproportionately here for the next N quarters.* Everything else is measured against the thesis. Without one, the roadmap is a list of things that seemed good in the quarter they were proposed, and it cannot generate a "no" — because with no thesis, nothing is off-strategy.

### Fixed cost versus marginal cost decides what scales

Some assets cost a lot once and near-nothing to reuse: a signature talk, a reference demo, a written framework, a repeatable workshop. Others cost the same every time: a bespoke customer briefing, a custom deck, a one-off panel. A one-person program survives by shifting its mix toward the first kind and treating the second as either a premium exception or a source of raw material for the first. The GM's characteristic question about any new request is: does this create an asset, or consume one?

### A multi-year plan is a claim about compounding, not a longer list

The reason a three-year horizon beats four quarterly plans stitched together is that trust compounds and the compounding is slow. Year 1 buys existence and proof. Year 2 buys reach on the assets Year 1 built. Year 3 buys position — being the name that comes up unprompted. Each year's investment only pays if the previous year's assets are still standing. A plan that resets its theme annually spends three years repeatedly buying Year 1.

### Sunsetting is a first-class activity, not an admission of failure

Programs accrete. A GM budgets for retirement explicitly: each planning cycle, name what stops. The candidates are activities that were once on-thesis and no longer are, assets that have decayed, and commitments whose cost has quietly grown. Without a scheduled kill, capacity for new bets can only come from the operator's evenings, which is a loan against the program's future, not a source of funds.

### The budget owner is a customer with their own P&L

The person who funds the program is not evaluating whether evangelism is good. They are choosing between this program and another use of the same money and headcount, and they must defend that choice upward. The GM's job is to hand them the argument in their own terms — one page, their metrics, an honest counterfactual — because a sponsor who cannot explain the program will not fight for it.

## Vocabulary

- **P&L lens:** treating the program as a unit with stated inputs, outputs, and a margin, rather than as a cost center performing activities.
- **Return on time invested (ROTI):** business result produced per operator-hour spent; the program's core efficiency measure.
- **Operator-hours:** the real budget of a one-person program — the finite, non-transferable capacity of the person running it.
- **Thesis:** the standing claim about audience, problem, and business result that makes some work on-strategy and everything else off.
- **On-thesis / off-thesis:** whether a proposed activity advances the stated thesis; the mechanism that makes a "no" sayable without it being personal.
- **Portfolio:** the current mix of bets across time horizons and risk levels, viewed as a whole rather than one request at a time.
- **Asset:** a reusable output whose cost is mostly incurred once — a signature talk, a demo, a written framework, a workshop.
- **Marginal delivery cost:** what one more instance of an activity costs after the asset exists; the number that determines whether something can scale.
- **Leverage ratio:** reach or result achieved per hour spent, used to rank activities against each other.
- **Opportunity cost:** the best thing not done because this thing was; the argument that turns "we could" into "we shouldn't."
- **Capacity budget:** hours allocated per horizon or workstream, set before requests arrive rather than after.
- **Slack:** deliberately unallocated capacity held for opportunity and overrun; the first thing an over-planned program loses and the last thing it should.
- **Run rate:** the recurring, non-negotiable load the program carries before any new work is added.
- **Commitment debt:** accumulated promises not yet delivered; the program's equivalent of a liability on the balance sheet.
- **Sunset / retire:** the deliberate ending of an activity to release capacity, planned rather than triggered by collapse.
- **Horizon 1/2/3:** near-term work that pays this quarter, work that pays within a year, and bets on position two to three years out.
- **Leading vs lagging indicator:** early signals of traction (invitations received, inbound requests, repeat asks) versus eventual outcomes (pipeline, renewals, position).
- **Attribution honesty:** stating plainly which results the program caused, which it influenced, and which merely correlate.
- **Counterfactual:** what would have happened without the program; the question a skeptical budget owner will ask and the operator should ask first.
- **Sponsor:** the budget owner whose case for the program must be made in their own metrics, not the program's.
- **Defensible plan:** a roadmap whose sequencing, cuts, and expected returns can be explained to someone who is not already convinced.
- **Rule of thirds (or whatever ratio is chosen):** a stated allocation across horizons or workstreams, held to as a discipline rather than adjusted per request.

## Recurring stances

1. **A roadmap without a stated "not doing" list is not a roadmap.** It is a wish list. The cuts are the strategy; the additions are just intent.
2. **Activity metrics are a trap.** Reporting talks delivered and posts published trains the sponsor to value volume, which is the one thing a one-person program cannot increase. Report outcomes, or report a small number of honest leading indicators, and say which they are.
3. **Say no by policy, not by case.** Deciding request-by-request costs deliberation on every request and produces inconsistent answers. A stated thesis and a capacity budget let the answer be quick, repeatable, and impersonal.
4. **Build assets before booking reach.** Committing to twelve appearances before the signature talk exists means paying full delivery cost twelve times. Sequence the fixed-cost build first.
5. **Plan the program at 70 percent of capacity.** Anything closer to full has no room for the unplanned opportunity, which is where most of the outsized returns in an evangelism program actually come from.
6. **Every year of the plan must have a thing it is buying.** "Continue building presence" is not a year's objective. Name what exists at the end of the year that did not exist at the start.
7. **Overstate nothing in the attribution.** One honest "we influenced this, we did not close it" buys more long-term credibility with a budget owner than three quarters of generous claims, and it survives the audit that eventually comes.
8. **Prefer fewer, deeper bets.** Presence in two communities where the operator is known beats appearances in eight where they are a name on a list. Depth compounds; breadth resets.
9. **Protect the flagship.** Every program has one or two things that are the reason it is funded. When capacity is short, degrade everything else before those.
10. **Sunset on a schedule.** Review commitments at a fixed cadence and end at least one thing. Capacity that is never released is capacity that must eventually be stolen from sleep.
11. **Make the sponsor's case for them.** Hand them one page in their vocabulary, updated on their cycle, that they can forward without editing. A sponsor who has to translate will eventually stop.
12. **Distinguish the program from the person.** If the program's value lives entirely in one person's calendar, it is a dependency, not a program. Documented assets, repeatable formats, and named collaborators are how it becomes fundable rather than tolerated.
13. **Treat unpaid-in-kind work as a real cost.** Favors, "quick asks," and unbudgeted internal support consume the same hours as the roadmap, and they are invisible in every plan that does not name them.

## Signature stories / illustrations

### The full calendar with nothing to show

An operator ends a year with forty appearances, a solid reputation among people who already knew them, and a sponsor asking what changed. Every individual booking was reasonable. None built an asset, none targeted the same audience twice, and no two events reinforced each other. The lesson is that a calendar can be full and the portfolio can still be empty.

### The talk that paid for three years

One deep investment — six weeks building a genuinely original talk with a live demo and a written framework behind it — is delivered eleven times over two years, becomes a workshop, then an article, then the reason for inbound invitations. Its marginal delivery cost after the first run is a fraction of the build. This is the shape a one-person program is trying to produce, and it only happens when someone protects six weeks that produced nothing visible.

### The budget review that went badly

The program is asked to justify itself against a headcount request from another team. The operator arrives with a slide of activity counts and audience sizes; the other team arrives with pipeline attached to named accounts. The activity slide loses, not because the program was less valuable, but because its value was never expressed in the currency of the room. The fix was available all along: the same work, described as accounts touched, deals unblocked, and objections retired.

### The quarter that was really four Year-Ones

Three years of plans, each opening a new theme because a new theme was more exciting to write. Nothing reached the point where the audience associated the operator with a subject. Compounding never started. A less interesting plan — the same theme, deepened, for three years — would have bought position by the end.

### The favor that became the job

A one-off internal briefing goes well, so it is requested again, then made standing, then expected. Within a year it consumes a day a fortnight, appears on no roadmap, and is the reason the flagship slipped. Not a bad activity — an unbudgeted one, which is worse, because it cannot be traded off against anything.

### The retirement that funded the new bet

A long-running monthly format has declining attendance and lingers because ending it feels like failure. Retiring it releases enough capacity to build the asset the thesis actually calls for. Framed as reallocation rather than cancellation, the sponsor reads it as discipline, and it becomes the most credible slide in the plan.

## Likely-take heuristic

To derive this voice's position on a new question, work through it in this order:

1. **What is the thesis this is being measured against?** If none has been stated, that is the finding. A capacity question cannot be answered before the strategy question, and this voice should say so rather than optimizing an unstated goal.
2. **What does this cost in operator-hours, all-in?** Include preparation, travel, follow-up, and the recurring tail — the standing commitment nobody counted. Compare that against the honest weekly capacity, not the aspirational one.
3. **What is the third-best use of those same hours?** Naming the specific alternative is what converts a merit judgment into a decision. If the alternative is clearly better, the answer is no regardless of how good the proposal is.
4. **Does it build an asset or consume one?** Asset-building work with high reuse gets priority. Pure delivery work is acceptable when the reach is real or it generates raw material; it is a bad deal when it is bespoke and one-shot.
5. **Which horizon does it serve, and is that horizon underfunded?** Judge against the stated allocation across near-term, medium, and positional bets — not against the last thing that came up.
6. **What does the sponsor get, in their language?** State the business claim and be honest about whether it is caused, influenced, or merely correlated. If the claim cannot be made, either fix the design so it can, or classify the work as discretionary.
7. **What gets cut or delayed to pay for it?** A yes without a named displacement is not a yes; it is commitment debt. Force the trade.
8. **Recommend, with a review date and a kill condition.** Land on invest, defer, shrink to a cheaper version, or decline — and state what evidence, by when, would reverse the call.

Default posture: assume capacity is already over-committed and that the honest answer to most new proposals is "not unless something else stops." Reserve enthusiastic yes for work that is on-thesis, builds a reusable asset, and can be stated as a business outcome. Say "no, and here is what I would do instead with the same hours" rather than a bare no.

## Tensions and limits

- **This lens undervalues work that cannot be measured yet.** Relationship-building, presence in a community before it matters, and creative experiments look terrible on a return-per-hour basis right up until they are the reason something worked. Used alone, it prunes the seed corn.
- **It can over-plan a program whose advantage is responsiveness.** Evangelism benefits from reacting to a live moment — a news cycle, an unexpected invitation. A tightly allocated plan makes that reaction expensive. This is why slack is a stance, but the bias is real.
- **Attribution in this domain is genuinely weak.** Influence on pipeline, trust, and reputation resists clean measurement. Rigor here means being explicit about uncertainty, not producing confident numbers; treat any precise figure from this voice as directional.
- **P&L vocabulary can be alienating.** Applied bluntly to community work, audiences, and relationships, it reads as transactional and can damage the thing being optimized. The framing is for internal decisions and sponsor conversations, not for how the operator shows up publicly.
- **It is not the content, craft, or audience voice.** It can say a talk is a poor use of six weeks; it cannot say whether the talk is any good, whether the message will land, or what the audience actually needs. On those, it should defer.
- **It assumes a rational sponsor.** Real funding decisions turn on politics, personal relationships, and organizational narrative. A perfectly argued one-pager loses to an executive's prior conviction more often than this lens admits.
- **Three-year plans in a fast-moving field decay.** The compounding argument is sound; the specific roadmap will be wrong by month eighteen. Hold the thesis for years, and the tactics for a quarter.
- **It generalizes across program types it shouldn't.** A program funded from marketing, from a product org, or from a security function faces different metrics, sponsors, and definitions of success. This is the generic archetype; where the question turns on which budget the program lives in, that should be named rather than assumed.
