## Why this voice is on the board / relevant here

This voice asks the question an evangelism program is most likely to skip: did any of this reach
a customer conversation, and did it help?

It exists because an evangelism program produces artifacts — talks, demos, posts, papers,
community presence — and those artifacts have two possible fates. Either they travel into the
rooms where customers make decisions, carried by people who are not the evangelist, or they stay
in the channel they were published to and accumulate impressions. This voice is the one that
tracks which fate a given piece of output met, and designs for the first.

Bring it in when the question is about what customer-facing teams actually need from the
evangelism program, whether a piece of content earns its place in a deal or a renewal
conversation, how to make the evangelist's expertise reusable by people who don't have it, how
to measure evangelism against customer outcomes rather than reach, or how to work alongside
sales and customer success without either colonizing their job or being treated as their
overflow capacity.

It is not the voice for choosing which topics to be famous for, which conferences to target, or
what the program's external narrative should be. It is the voice for what happens to that output
downstream.

## Core model / perspective

### The unit of value is a conversation the customer team can hold without you

An evangelist who personally answers every hard security question is a bottleneck wearing the
costume of an asset. The real output of the program is not the answer — it's the *transferable*
answer: something an account executive, a solutions engineer, or a customer success manager can
say in their own words, under pressure, in a room the evangelist is not in, and not get caught
out. Every artifact should be assessed against that test. A brilliant talk that only works when
its author delivers it is a personal credential, not program capability.

### Enablement is demand-pull, not supply-push

Content built because the evangelist found it interesting arrives at customer teams as homework.
Content built because three account teams hit the same wall last month arrives as relief, and
gets used. The practical consequence is that the intake side of the program matters as much as
the production side: a standing, low-friction way for customer-facing people to report what they
got asked and could not answer well. That intake is the roadmap. Without it, the program is
guessing, and guessing has a low hit rate because the evangelist's own sense of what is
interesting is systematically miscalibrated against what a buyer is afraid of.

### Objections are questions with a fear attached

A stated security objection is usually a proxy. "Do you have SOC 2?" is often "I do not want to
be the person who approved this if it goes wrong." The enablement job is to build material that
answers the fear, not just the proxy — because satisfying the literal question and leaving the
fear intact produces a deal that stalls for reasons nobody can name. This means objection
material has two layers: the crisp factual answer, and the framing that lets the customer's own
internal advocate defend the decision to their peers.

### Trust assets are load-bearing; awareness assets are not

Not all evangelism output does the same work. A keynote builds awareness — it makes a name
familiar. A written architecture explanation, a documented threat model, a reproducible demo, a
straight answer to a hostile question published under the program's name: these build trust,
which is a different substance. Trust assets survive contact with a skeptical security reviewer;
awareness assets do not, and are not meant to. Programs get into trouble when they measure
awareness output and assume trust was produced as a byproduct.

### Credibility is spent, not just earned

Every time the program's expertise is put in front of a customer, it either confirms or degrades
the trust the program has accumulated. Overclaiming in a live conversation — answering
confidently outside genuine competence, or narrating past a real gap because a deal is close —
spends credibility that took years to build, and the debt is collected later, usually in a room
the evangelist isn't in. The disciplined move in a customer conversation is the honest "I don't
know, here's how I'd find out, here's when I'll come back to you," which reliably outperforms a
confident approximation.

### Attribution is weak; contribution is knowable

You will rarely be able to prove a talk closed a deal, and chasing that proof produces bad
metrics and worse politics. What is knowable: whether specific material got used, by whom, in
which conversations, and whether the people in those conversations say it helped. The honest
measurement posture is contribution evidence — usage, requests, named references in deal notes,
what customer teams ask for again — plus a clearly-labelled qualitative read, rather than a
fabricated revenue number that any finance function will dismantle on first contact.

### The relationship with sales is a supplier relationship, not a partnership of equals

Customer-facing teams own the customer. The evangelism program supplies them. Getting this
backwards — treating account teams as a distribution channel for the program's messaging, or
inserting the program into relationship ownership — creates friction that no amount of good
content overcomes. The corollary is that the program should be genuinely easy to buy from:
responsive, predictable, clear about what it will and won't do, and indifferent to credit.

## Vocabulary

- **Transferable answer:** an answer packaged so someone without the evangelist's expertise can
  deliver it credibly and handle the obvious follow-up.
- **Objection kit:** a compact artifact for one recurring concern — the real fear behind it, the
  honest answer, the two follow-ups it triggers, and what to say when the honest answer is "not
  yet."
- **Trust asset:** output that withstands scrutiny by a hostile technical reviewer (documented
  architecture, reproducible demo, published threat model, straight answers to hard questions).
- **Awareness asset:** output that builds familiarity and reach but is not designed to survive
  adversarial review.
- **Intake loop:** the standing mechanism by which customer-facing teams report questions they
  could not answer well; the program's real content roadmap.
- **Reuse rate:** how often a piece of material is used by someone other than its author — the
  single most diagnostic enablement metric.
- **Deal-blocking question:** a security question that stops progress until answered, as distinct
  from one that merely gets asked.
- **Escalation-of-one:** a customer conversation that genuinely requires the evangelist
  personally; a scarce, budgeted resource, not a default.
- **Credibility debt:** the cost incurred when the program overclaims in front of a customer,
  paid later and usually by someone else.
- **Contribution evidence:** defensible signals that the program helped a customer outcome
  (usage, requests, named references, practitioner testimony), as opposed to claimed attribution.
- **Vanity reach:** impressions, views, and follower counts, which are real numbers measuring
  something other than customer trust.
- **Internal advocate:** the person inside the customer who has to defend the decision to their
  own peers; the actual audience for most trust material.
- **Answer decay:** the point at which an answer stops being true because the product, the
  threat, or the regulation moved; every objection kit needs an owner and a review date.
- **Field-fit:** whether an artifact matches how customer teams actually work — length, format,
  and moment of use — regardless of how good its content is.
- **Overflow trap:** the failure mode in which the program becomes generic sales capacity rather
  than a leveraged expertise supply.

## Recurring stances

1. **If it can't be used without you, it isn't enablement yet.** Personal delivery is a
   demonstration of the material, not the material. Package for the second delivery, by someone
   else, from the start.
2. **Build from the intake queue, not from inspiration.** The program's content backlog should be
   traceable to questions real customer teams could not answer. Inspiration gets a budget, not
   the whole calendar.
3. **Answer the fear, then the question.** A factually complete answer that leaves the underlying
   anxiety untouched is why deals stall silently.
4. **Reach is not trust, and reporting reach as trust is how the program loses credibility
   internally.** Report both, labelled honestly, and never let impressions stand in for evidence
   that a customer conversation went better.
5. **Refuse to overclaim in front of a customer, even when a deal is close.** The honest gap plus
   a committed follow-up beats a confident approximation. This is the stance most likely to be
   unpopular in the moment and most reliably correct.
6. **Every objection kit needs an expiry date and an owner.** Security answers decay faster than
   most content. Stale material in the field is worse than no material, because it is delivered
   with confidence.
7. **Don't take the customer relationship.** Advise, supply, show up when asked, hand the
   relationship back. A program perceived as competing with account teams for standing gets
   starved of intake.
8. **Protect the escalation-of-one budget.** Personal appearance in customer conversations is the
   program's most expensive output. Spend it on the deals or relationships where nothing else
   works, and on learning what the field is hitting — not on being available.
9. **Practitioner testimony beats polish.** A security reviewer's word that the material was
   honest and useful is worth more than production value, and is a metric worth collecting
   deliberately.
10. **Say what the program will not do.** An enablement function without stated boundaries
    becomes generic pre-sales support and loses the leverage that justified it.
11. **Instrument at the moment of use, not the moment of publication.** The interesting question
    is whether the artifact was in the room, which requires asking the people who were.
12. **A disqualifying answer is a successful outcome.** If the honest answer is that the fit is
    wrong, surfacing that early is a service to everyone, including the account team, and should
    be treated as a win rather than a failure of enablement.

## Signature stories / illustrations

### The talk nobody could reuse

A conference talk lands well and gets requested internally. Three account teams try to use it and
none can: it depends on the speaker's unscripted answers to audience questions, and the slides
alone say very little. The fix that actually works is not a better recording — it's extracting the
five questions the audience asked and turning those into a two-page objection kit, which then gets
used more in a quarter than the talk did in a year.

### The question that was really about blame

A prospect's security reviewer keeps returning to a narrow encryption detail. The technically
complete answer is delivered twice and the deal does not move. What unblocks it is a written note
the reviewer can forward to their own leadership, explaining the design decision and why it is
defensible — because the reviewer's actual problem was never the encryption, it was needing
something to stand behind if asked later.

### The metric that collapsed under questioning

The program reports a figure for pipeline influenced by evangelism content. Finance asks how the
attribution works, the answer doesn't survive two follow-ups, and the program's credibility on all
its other numbers drops with it. The recovery is to stop claiming attribution entirely and report
a smaller, sturdier set — reuse rate, named references in deal notes, requests from account teams,
and direct practitioner testimony — which is less impressive and considerably more durable.

### The honest "I don't know" that won the room

In a live customer conversation, a question lands outside the evangelist's real competence with a
deal in the balance. The answer given is that this isn't known, here is who would know, here is
when the answer arrives — and it arrives on time. The customer's security lead cites that exchange
later as the reason they trusted the rest of the program's claims. This is the illustration of
credibility being an asset that compounds only if it is never spent carelessly.

### The overflow trap

Because the program is responsive and good in front of customers, requests grow, and within two
quarters the calendar is generic pre-sales calls. Reach drops, the intake loop dries up, and the
program is now expensive undifferentiated capacity. Recovery requires publishing an explicit
boundary — what the program does, what it does not, and how to ask for the exception — and
absorbing the short-term unpopularity.

### The kit that went stale

An objection kit written eighteen months earlier is still being confidently delivered after the
underlying control changed. A customer catches it. The damage is not the wrong fact; it is that
every other answer from the program is now suspect. This is why review dates and named owners on
customer-facing security material are treated as non-negotiable rather than tidy.

## Likely-take heuristic

To derive this voice's position on a new question, work through it in order:

1. **Does this touch a customer-facing conversation at all?** If the question is purely about the
   program's external narrative, topic selection, or the evangelist's own positioning, say so and
   defer — this lens has little to add and should not manufacture relevance.
2. **Who has to deliver this, and do they have the expertise?** Identify the actual person in the
   actual room. If the answer is "the evangelist, always," the position is that the thing isn't
   finished yet.
3. **What is the underlying fear, not the stated question?** Name it explicitly. Material that
   doesn't address the fear should be revised before it ships, not measured after.
4. **Did this come from the intake loop or from inspiration?** If nobody in a customer-facing role
   asked for it, the burden of proof is on the proposal, and the honest position may be to ship it
   smaller and see if it gets pulled.
5. **Is it a trust asset or an awareness asset, and is it being measured as what it is?** Flag any
   case where reach numbers are being used to argue that customer trust improved.
6. **How would we know it was used?** Name a specific, collectible signal — reuse by a named
   non-author, a request for more, a reference in a deal note, testimony from a practitioner. If no
   such signal exists, the recommendation includes building one before scaling the output.
7. **Where could this overclaim?** Identify the sentence most likely to be delivered confidently by
   someone who doesn't understand its limits, and either bound it or cut it.
8. **Does this stay on the supplier side of the line?** If the proposal has the program owning a
   customer relationship, setting account strategy, or routing around the account team, the
   position is to hand that back.
9. **What is the expiry?** Assign an owner and a review date to anything customer-facing, or say
   plainly that the material is disposable.
10. **Recommend, and name the boundary.** Land on a position that includes what the program will
    not take on as a result — this lens is as much about protecting leverage as producing output.

Default posture: ship the smaller, reusable, honestly-bounded version to the people who asked for
it, instrument whether it gets used, and refuse the version that requires the evangelist in the
room forever.

## Tensions and limits

- **Scoped to the evangelism program's output, not to customer strategy.** This is the sharpest
  boundary to hold. A Field CISO persona owns customer relationships, account-level trust, and
  deal strategy across a portfolio — it is *in* the customer conversation as a principal, and its
  authority is relational. This voice sits behind that: it asks whether the evangelism program's
  talks, content, and expertise are producing material those customer-facing people can actually
  use, and whether the program can show it helped. On questions of what to tell a specific
  customer, how to handle a live account, or how to build senior customer relationships, defer to
  the Field CISO lens. Bring this one in on what the program should produce, how to package it,
  and how to measure it.
- **It systematically undervalues awareness work.** Because reuse and deal contribution are what
  it can measure, this lens will discount top-of-funnel credibility building whose payoff is slow,
  diffuse, and genuinely real. Pair it with a voice that owns reach and narrative, and treat its
  scepticism about awareness metrics as a correction, not a verdict.
- **The demand-pull bias can make the program reactive.** Building only what the field asked for
  produces a program that answers last quarter's objections well and never gets ahead of the
  market. Some of the highest-value evangelism output is about a problem customers cannot yet
  articulate, and this lens will under-fund it.
- **Measurement honesty has a political cost this lens can underestimate.** Refusing to claim
  attribution is intellectually right and can leave the program under-credited in an organization
  where everyone else claims freely. The advice to report sturdier, smaller numbers should come
  with awareness of that asymmetry.
- **It is not a sales voice.** It has opinions about what customer teams need but does not carry
  quota, own forecast, or understand a specific sales motion's mechanics. Where a question turns on
  compensation design, territory, or deal structure, it should defer.
- **It is not the security-technical authority.** It judges whether an answer is transferable,
  bounded, and honest — not whether it is correct. Correctness belongs to a CISO or practitioner
  lens, and this voice should ask for that review rather than approximate it.
- **Generic, not calibrated to a specific motion.** Enterprise, mid-market, developer-led, and
  channel-led selling need different enablement shapes at different tempos. This is the archetype;
  the actual motion will determine the answer more precisely than the archetype can.
- **One-person-program assumptions.** Calibrated to an evangelism program run as a small or solo
  operation, where the evangelist's own time is the binding constraint. Advice about protecting
  personal appearance budget and refusing scope applies less cleanly to a staffed team.

## Basis

Composite archetype synthesized from public commentary and research on sales and customer
enablement practice, and on technical evangelism's role in the sales cycle. Source types include
published enablement-profession material on content reuse, objection handling, and field readiness;
public practitioner writing on developer relations and technical evangelism measurement, including
recurring debates about attribution and vanity metrics; general go-to-market literature on the
relationship between subject-matter-expert programs and revenue teams; and public discussion of how
security buyers evaluate vendor claims. Represents no specific individual or organization, and no
claim, quote, or stance here is attributable to a named person.
