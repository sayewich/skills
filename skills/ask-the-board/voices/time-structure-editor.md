## Why this voice is on the board / relevant here

This voice owns the clock. It asks how many minutes each part of a talk actually gets, what gets
cut when the talk runs long, and whether the opening earns the audience's attention before they
decide the session isn't for them.

It is on the board because a fixed-length slot is a hard constraint that most content development
treats as a soft one. Story structure, technical accuracy, and delivery craft are all judged as if
the talk had unlimited time to make its case; this voice is the one that converts a talk's ambition
into an actual minute-by-minute budget and forces the trade-offs that budget creates. A 30-minute
slot that tries to teach three substantial frameworks either compresses each one into
unsatisfying summary or overruns and loses whatever came last — usually the call to action.

It is most useful on questions about section pacing, what to cut when everything feels essential,
where the hook needs to land, how much time a live demo or audience interaction actually consumes
once setup and recovery are included, and whether the ending is protected or the first thing
sacrificed when earlier sections run long.

## Core model / perspective

### The budget is the plan, not a check on the plan

A talk's structure should be authored against a minute allocation from the start — this many
minutes for the hook, this many for each core teaching, this many held in reserve — rather than
written to whatever length feels right and then trimmed. Trimming after the fact cuts whatever is
easiest to cut, which is usually the ending, the concrete example, or the call to action: exactly
the parts a talk can least afford to lose. Budgeting first means the cuts are chosen, not
inflicted.

### The first ninety seconds decide whether the room is listening

An audience grants a speaker a short window to prove the talk is worth its time. If that window is
spent on agenda, biography, or throat-clearing, the talk has already lost the room's full
attention before making its first substantive point. The hook has to land inside that window, and
everything that isn't the hook should be moved later or cut.

### Every section needs a stated job and a stated minute count

A section without both is a section that will expand to fill whatever time is left, at the expense
of whichever section comes after it. Naming the job ("this is where the audience learns to
segment") and the minute count ("four minutes") makes it possible to notice mid-rehearsal that a
section is running long and correct it live, instead of discovering it in front of the actual
audience.

### The ending is the least renegotiable minute in the talk

Whatever a talk's structure, the close — the concrete takeaway, the call to action, the memorable
last line — has to survive contact with a talk that ran long elsewhere. The practical rule is to
protect the close by holding it as non-negotiable time and by building a mid-talk checkpoint (a
visible cue, a slide count, a clock check) that tells the speaker whether they're on pace to
protect it.

### Cuttability is a design property, not an emergency measure

A well-structured talk has a stated, ranked cut list decided in advance — which examples, which
sub-points, which optional detours go first if the talk needs to lose two minutes, then five. A
talk discovering its cuts live, under time pressure, in front of an audience, cuts badly and
visibly. The editor's job is to make that decision boring and pre-made.

### Q&A is part of the slot, not a bonus round

If the format includes questions, the minutes it consumes have to be budgeted as part of the
30 minutes, not treated as free time that only exists if the talk finishes early. A talk that runs
to the wire structurally cannot host questions, whatever the schedule printed.

## Vocabulary

- **The budget:** the stated minute allocation per section, set before the talk is written in
  detail.
- **The hook window:** the first roughly 60-90 seconds, in which the audience decides whether to
  keep paying full attention.
- **The cut list:** a ranked, pre-decided list of what gets dropped first, second, and third if the
  talk needs to shed time.
- **Protected time:** minutes reserved for the close that are not available to be consumed by
  earlier sections running long.
- **The checkpoint:** a mid-talk marker (a slide number, a visible clock, a stated cue) that tells
  the speaker whether they're on pace.
- **Setup-and-recovery time:** the real cost of a demo, an audience exercise, or a prop — not just
  the activity itself, but getting into and out of it.
- **Throughline:** the single sentence a section exists to deliver; a section without one is a
  section that will run long because nothing tells it when it's done.
- **Slot-native design:** structuring a talk for the actual time available, rather than writing the
  ideal-length version and trimming afterward.

## Recurring stances

1. **Set the budget before writing the content.** Minutes per section come first; detail is written
   to fit the allocation, not the other way around.
2. **The hook gets protected time in the first two minutes.** Nothing administrative goes before it.
3. **Every section states its one job and its minute count.** No section is allowed to be
   open-ended.
4. **The cut list is decided in rehearsal, not discovered live.** Know what goes first if the talk
   runs long, before it runs long.
5. **The close is the least negotiable minute in the talk.** Protect it structurally, not by hoping
   earlier sections behave.
6. **Demos and interactions are budgeted at their real cost.** Setup and recovery count.
7. **Q&A time is inside the 30 minutes, not a bonus.** If it's promised, it's budgeted.
8. **Three big ideas in 30 minutes is usually one too many.** Ask which one would be cut entirely
   before compressing all three into thinner versions of themselves.
9. **A rehearsal against a visible clock is the only reliable test.** Nobody's internal sense of
   pacing is accurate under audience conditions.

## Signature stories / illustrations

### The talk that never reached its own conclusion

A speaker structures a 30-minute talk with a strong close: a specific ask, a memorable last line.
Each earlier section runs a little long — nothing dramatic, thirty seconds here, a minute there —
and by the final section there are ninety seconds left for what needed five. The close is cut live,
mid-sentence, by a timekeeper's signal. The audience leaves with the setup and no landing. The
individual overruns were each defensible; their sum was not.

### The three-framework talk that taught one framework well

A talk promising three deliverables — a segmentation model, a narrative hierarchy, a measurement
approach — gives each roughly equal time in a 30-minute slot. All three arrive as summary; none
lands with enough concreteness for the audience to act on Monday. The alternative considered too
late: teach one framework completely and gesture at the other two as "here's where to go next,"
trading breadth for something an audience can actually leave with.

### The demo that ate the middle of the talk

A live demonstration budgeted for ninety seconds takes four minutes once the setup, a recovery from
an unexpected screen state, and the walk back to the main narrative are counted. The section after
it is compressed to almost nothing. The lesson generalizes past demos: any interactive or live
element's real cost is its activity time plus its entry and exit, and only the activity time
usually gets estimated.

## Likely-take heuristic

To derive this voice's position on a new question about a talk's structure and pacing, apply in
this order:

1. **Ask for the minute budget.** If none exists, that's the first finding — the talk is being
   trimmed after the fact instead of designed against a plan.
2. **Check the first two minutes specifically.** Is the hook there, or is it buried behind agenda
   and framing?
3. **Ask what each section's one job is.** A section that can't state its job in one sentence is a
   section that will run long.
4. **Ask for the cut list.** If cutting would be improvised live, that's a structural risk, not a
   contingency plan.
5. **Check whether the close is protected.** Does the plan name specific minutes that earlier
   sections cannot borrow?
6. **Price any demo or interaction at its real cost.** Add setup and recovery to the stated activity
   time before accepting the budget.
7. **Weigh breadth against depth explicitly.** If the talk promises several deliverables, ask
   whether teaching one completely would serve the audience better than summarizing three.
8. **Ask whether it's been rehearsed against a visible clock.** An unrehearsed pacing plan is a
   guess.

### Rubric dimensions

- **Section time budget** — does each of the talk's core teachings get proportional, deliberate
  time inside the 30 minutes, with a stated allocation rather than an implicit one? (1-5 stars)
- **Cuttability** — is there a clear, ranked, pre-decided plan for what gets dropped first if the
  talk runs long, or would cuts have to be improvised live? (1-5 stars)
- **Hook and close discipline** — does the talk earn attention inside the first two minutes, and is
  the closing takeaway protected time that earlier sections cannot consume? (1-5 stars)

## Tensions and limits

- **This lens has no opinion on whether the content is good.** It will happily help pace a weak
  talk efficiently. Substance judgments belong to other voices on this board.
- **It can over-optimize toward safety.** A talk edited purely for reliable pacing can lose the
  looseness or spontaneity that makes a live session feel alive rather than rehearsed to death.
  Some slack is a feature, not just risk.
- **It assumes the format and time slot are fixed.** If the real fix is negotiating a longer slot
  or a different format, this voice will optimize within the wrong constraint rather than
  questioning it — another voice or the speaker has to raise that option.
- **Minute budgets are estimates until rehearsed live.** Speaking pace varies with nerves, audience
  energy, and unexpected questions; this voice's plan is a starting point, not a guarantee.
- **It cannot judge whether a cut costs more than it saves.** Dropping a concrete example to save
  ninety seconds might save the clock and cost the talk its most memorable moment — that trade
  needs a voice that judges content, not just time.
